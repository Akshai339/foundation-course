# Rubrics and Self-Critique

How to grade your own work without lying to yourself.

← back to [[00 Start Here]]

---

## The problem with self-assessment

You are a visual designer. Your eye is trained and fast. This is an asset and a trap: **you will be able to tell whether your work looks good long before you can tell whether it works.** Looking good will feel like being done.

Every rubric below is deliberately weighted *away* from execution quality, because execution is the thing you least need help with.

---

## The universal rubric

Grade any piece of product work on five axes, 1–5. Total /25.

| Axis | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Framing** | Solves the problem as stated | Restated the problem more precisely | Found the real problem underneath the stated one, and can show why |
| **Structure** | Screens exist | Screens follow a flow | Flow is derived from a task analysis; states and branches are complete |
| **Rationale** | "It felt right" | Can point at a principle | Can name the principle, the tradeoff, and what you gave up |
| **Craft** | Inconsistent | Consistent and clean | Systematic — spacing, type, colour all derive from a stated logic |
| **Failure** | Happy path only | Errors handled | Empty / loading / error / partial / wrong / offline all designed |

**Your predicted profile in Week 1:** Craft 5, everything else 2.
**Target profile by Week 12:** everything ≥ 4.

If Craft is your highest score in Week 10, something has gone wrong — you've been retreating to your comfort zone.

## The five questions to ask any screen

Run these before you show anything to anyone. In order.

1. **What is the user trying to do here, in their words?** If you can't say it in one sentence without product jargon, the screen has no job.
2. **What must they know, remember, or decide to proceed?** Each item is cognitive load. Each one is a candidate for removal.
3. **What happens when this goes wrong?** If you don't have an answer, the design is half-finished.
4. **What did I make worse?** Every decision trades something. If you can't name the cost, you haven't made a decision — you've made a guess.
5. **Would this survive real data?** Longest name, empty list, 400 items, a German translation, a 3G connection.

## The critique protocol

Use this on your own work weekly, and with me via `/product-design-course critique`.

**Set-up (you provide):**
- The job the design is doing
- The constraint you were working under
- The *specific* thing you want critiqued — "is this hierarchy readable" not "thoughts?"

**Critique (three passes, in this order — do not let it start at pass 3):**

| Pass | Question | Why first |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Structural** | Is this the right set of screens/steps at all? | If the structure is wrong, everything downstream is wasted effort |
| 2. **Interaction** | Within this structure, is each step clear, forgiving, low-load? | |
| 3. **Visual** | Now — hierarchy, spacing, type, colour | Starting here is the #1 way critiques go wrong |

**Receiving critique:** your only job is to *understand*, not to defend. Write down every point before responding to any of them. The instinct to explain is the instinct that stops you hearing.

**A rule worth internalising:** when someone gives you a solution ("make this a dropdown"), your job is to find the *problem underneath it* ("you found the choice overwhelming"). Stakeholders are excellent problem detectors and unreliable solution generators. Treat their solutions as symptoms.

## Per-studio rubrics

### Studio A — The Teardown
- [ ] Every finding names a principle
- [ ] At least 3 findings are structural, not cosmetic
- [ ] Severity-rated, and the ratings are defensible
- [ ] The rebuild brief fixes **three things** and explicitly declines the rest
- [ ] You named what your redesign makes worse

**Fail condition:** the deliverable is a set of prettier screens.

### Studio B — Zero to One
- [ ] 3+ real interviews, with people who are not designers
- [ ] Something you believed at the start turned out to be wrong, and you wrote it down
- [ ] POV statement is specific enough to be falsifiable
- [ ] Flow diagram has branches and failure paths
- [ ] Screens are traceable to the flow, not designed first
- [ ] System is small, coherent, and every component is justified

**Fail condition:** the research confirmed everything you already believed. That's not research, it's ceremony.

### Studio C — The AI Feature
- [ ] The model's failure modes are enumerated before any screen exists
- [ ] There is a designed screen for *confidently wrong*
- [ ] Trust is calibrated — the UI does not oversell certainty
- [ ] User can steer, correct, and undo
- [ ] Motion is informational and spec'd with real durations/easing
- [ ] Mapped against the 18 HAX guidelines with gaps acknowledged
- [ ] HEART table exists and the metrics are actually instrumentable
- [ ] Case study section 3 ("what I had to figure out") is the longest section

**Fail condition:** it's a chat box with your brand's colours on it.

## Weekly self-check

Five minutes, end of each week. In your daily log:

1. What's the one idea from this week I'll still use in a year?
2. What did I not understand and quietly move past? *(Go back to it on day 7.)*
3. Where did I default to visual craft instead of doing the harder thinking?
4. What did I make this week that I can show someone?

Question 3 is the important one. Answer it honestly every single week.
