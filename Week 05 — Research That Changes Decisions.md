# Week 05 — Research That Changes Decisions

> **The week's thesis:** Most design research is theatre — it produces artifacts that decorate a deck and change nothing. Useful research is defined by one property: **at least one decision came out differently because of it.** This week is about the small number of methods that reliably do that, and how to run them without fooling yourself.

## Day 1 — Needfinding, and what people cannot tell you

### CONCEPT

The foundational fact of design research:

> **People are excellent reporters of their problems and unreliable predictors of their behaviour.**

Ask "would you use this?" and you get a socially-shaped guess. Ask "walk me through the last time you did this" and you get data. The entire craft is built on that distinction.

**Three things people genuinely cannot tell you:**

1. **What they'll do in future.** Intention is a weak predictor of behaviour, and interview settings inflate it — people are trying to be helpful. Every "yes I'd definitely use that" is worth approximately nothing.
2. **Why they did something.** People confabulate causes fluently and sincerely. The explanation they give is a plausible story constructed after the fact, not a readout of their reasoning. This isn't dishonesty; it's how introspection works.
3. **What they want that doesn't exist.** Not because of the misattributed "faster horses" quote, but because articulating an unmet need requires a vocabulary the need hasn't yet generated. They can describe the *pain* precisely. The solution is your job.

**What they're excellent at:**
- **Recounting specific past episodes**, especially recent and emotionally marked ones
- **Showing you their actual workarounds** — the spreadsheet, the notes app, the WhatsApp thread, the sticky note on the monitor
- **Reacting to something concrete** in front of them
- **Telling you what's annoying**, in vivid and useful detail

**So needfinding is built on three moves:**

**1. Ask about the past, specifically.** Not "how do you usually…" (that produces an idealised composite) but "tell me about the last time you…" (that produces an episode with real detail, including the messy parts).

**2. Look for workarounds — they are the highest-signal artifact in all of design research.** A workaround is proof of a need strong enough that someone paid a real cost to satisfy it, with no help from anyone. When you find someone maintaining a parallel spreadsheet next to your product, you have found a need with demonstrated willingness to pay in effort. That is worth more than fifty survey responses.

**3. Watch, don't just ask.** Contextual inquiry — observe the work happening in its real setting. People omit the parts that have become invisible to them, and those parts are frequently where the problem lives.

**A note on surveys, since your marketing background will reach for them:** surveys are good at measuring *known* variables across a *large* population. They are terrible at discovery, because you can only ask about what you already thought of, and the response options constrain the answer. Use surveys to size a problem you've already found qualitatively. Never to find one.

### SOURCE
Erika Hall, *Just Enough Research* — the chapters on interviewing and on the difference between what people say and do. Or free: [Stanford CS147's needfinding assignment brief](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs147/), which is a genuinely well-designed exercise you can run on yourself.

### DRILL
Write your **interview guide** for Studio B. 8–10 questions, and enforce these rules: every question is about a *specific past episode*; no question contains your hypothesis; no question can be answered yes/no; at least two questions hunt for workarounds. Then read each question aloud and delete any that you'd be disappointed to get an unexpected answer to — those are the leading ones.

## Day 2 — Running an interview without leading the witness

### CONCEPT

The interview is a skill, and it's mostly a skill of **restraint**.

**The structure that works** (30–45 minutes):

| Phase | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm-up** | 5 min | Establish that there are no wrong answers and you didn't build the thing. Get them talking. |
| **Context** | 5–10 min | Their role, their world, their week. Ground everything that follows. |
| **The episode** | 15–20 min | "Tell me about the last time…" Then dig. **This is the interview.** |
| **Artifacts** | 5 min | "Can you show me?" Screens, files, spreadsheets, notes. |
| **Wrap** | 5 min | "What haven't I asked about?" — routinely the best answer of the session. |

**The techniques that separate a good interview from a bad one:**

**Silence.** After they finish an answer, wait. Four seconds. It will feel unbearable and they will fill it, and the fill is usually better than the answer. This is the single highest-return technique in interviewing and it costs nothing but nerve.

**"Tell me more about that."** Content-free, so it can't lead. Deploy it whenever you feel a flicker of interest.

**The five whys, gently.** Ask why up to five times to get from behaviour to motivation. Do it warmly or it becomes an interrogation — "sorry, I want to make sure I understand, why was that…?"

**Ask for the last time, not the usual time.** "Usually" produces a sanitised composite. "Last Tuesday" produces reality including the parts they'd have edited out.

**Play back for confirmation.** "So if I'm hearing right, the annoying part isn't X, it's Y?" This both checks your understanding and frequently prompts a correction that's more precise than the original.

**The four ways to ruin an interview:**

1. **Leading.** "Don't you find it frustrating when…" You've just handed them the answer and they'll take it, because people are cooperative.
2. **Pitching.** The moment you explain your idea, the interview is over — they're now reacting to you rather than reporting on themselves. Save any concept exposure for the final five minutes, explicitly framed as a separate activity.
3. **Fixing.** Someone describes a problem and you say "oh, you can do that with…" You have just taught them a workaround and destroyed your data. Bite it back. Offer help *after* the recording stops.
4. **Filling silence.** See above. This is the most common failure and the easiest to fix.

**On recording:** always record with permission, always take sparse notes anyway (marking timestamps of interesting moments), never try to transcribe live. Splitting attention between listening and writing makes you a worse interviewer, and the good follow-up question is worth more than the neat note.

**A specific warning for you.** Marketing interviews are usually about *reaction* to messaging — you present and observe. Design interviews are about *observation* of behaviour with nothing presented. The muscle memory transfers badly. You will want to show them something. Don't.

### SOURCE
Steve Portigal, *Interviewing Users* — the best book on the actual mechanics. Free alternative: NN/g's [User Interviews](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/) plus their material on avoiding leading questions.

### DRILL
Run a **practice interview**, today, on a friend or colleague, about any mundane recurring task — how they plan meals, manage passwords, decide what to watch. 20 minutes, recorded. Then listen back and count: how many leading questions did you ask, how many times did you fill a silence, how many times did you offer a solution. The count will be higher than you expect. That's the point of doing it before the real ones.

## Day 3 — Synthesis: transcripts to insight without hallucinating

### CONCEPT

You now have hours of messy human talk. Synthesis is turning it into something that changes a decision — and it's the stage where research most often goes wrong, because it's where you can find whatever you were looking for.

**The process that keeps you honest:**

**1. Extract observations, not conclusions.** Go through each transcript and pull out *individual, atomic, quoted* things. One per sticky note (physical or FigJam). The rule: **an observation must be something the person said or did, not what you think it means.**

> ✓ "Keeps a separate spreadsheet of which clients have paid, updated weekly"
> ✗ "Frustrated by lack of payment tracking"

The second is an interpretation smuggled in as data, and once it's on the wall you'll never re-examine it.

**2. Affinity map.** Cluster the observations by similarity — **without predefined categories.** Let the groups emerge. Then name each group *after* it's formed. If you start with categories, you'll sort into your existing beliefs and learn nothing.

**3. Look for the three signals that matter:**
- **Repeated behaviours across people** — the strongest signal. Three of three doing the same odd thing is a pattern.
- **Contradictions** — where someone's stated belief conflicts with their observed behaviour. This gap is nearly always where the real insight lives.
- **Emotional spikes** — where the register changed. Frustration, relief, embarrassment, pride. Emotion marks what matters.

**4. Write findings, then insights.** A *finding* is what you observed. An *insight* is what it means and what follows. Keep them separate and labelled:

> **Finding:** All three keep a parallel record outside the tool. Two mentioned not trusting the tool's history after a past sync issue.
> **Insight:** The parallel spreadsheet isn't a feature gap — it's a *trust* artifact. Adding reporting features won't remove it. Making history verifiable might.

**Guarding against hallucinating patterns**, which you will do, because pattern-finding is what brains are for:

- **N is tiny.** Three interviews is enough to *find* things and never enough to *size* them. Say "two of three" and never "67% of users."
- **Count the disconfirmations too.** For every pattern, actively look for the interview that contradicts it, and record it.
- **Ask what would have changed your mind.** If nothing could have, it wasn't research.
- **The prediction check.** Go back to your dated hypothesis from Week 4 Day 6. What was wrong? *If nothing was wrong, be suspicious of yourself* — either you didn't listen, you asked leading questions, or you picked a problem you already understood. All three are worth knowing.

**On using AI for synthesis** — relevant to you and worth being precise about. Models are genuinely good at: transcription, first-pass clustering, finding repeated language across documents, and generating counter-hypotheses ("what's the strongest case that this pattern is noise?"). They are dangerous at: producing a confident synthesis that smooths over the contradictions, which are the valuable part. Use a model to *widen* (more candidate patterns, more counter-arguments) and use your own reading to *decide*. And never let a model see your hypothesis before it clusters — you'll get your hypothesis back, beautifully written.

### SOURCE
Goodman, Kuniavsky & Moed, *Observing the User Experience* — the synthesis chapters. Or free: NN/g's [Affinity Diagramming](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/affinity-diagram/).

### DRILL
Use your practice interview from yesterday. Extract 15–20 atomic observations onto stickies. Affinity map them with no predefined categories. Name the clusters. Write one finding and one insight, properly separated. Notice how much smaller the output is than the input — that compression is the work.

## Day 4 — Personas: why they mostly fail, and what to use instead

### CONCEPT

Personas were a genuinely good idea (Alan Cooper, *The Inmates Are Running the Asylum*) that got hollowed out into a ritual. Understanding *why* they fail teaches you more than using them.

**The original intent:** stop designing for "the user," an infinitely flexible abstraction who conveniently wants whatever you were going to build. Replace them with a specific, named, constrained person you can't argue with. Cooper's insight was that specificity *prevents* accommodation — you can't add a feature "for the user" if the user is Marcus, 34, who would never find it.

**Why they usually fail in practice:**

1. **They get built from demographics rather than behaviour.** "Sarah, 32, marketing manager, likes yoga and travel." None of that predicts a single design decision. Age and hobbies are noise; *behaviour under a constraint* is signal.
2. **They're built from assumption, not research.** A workshop persona is a written-down team consensus. It has the authority of research with none of the substance, which makes it worse than nothing.
3. **They're too flattering and too coherent.** Real users are inconsistent, distracted, partially informed, and doing three things at once. Personas are always competent and always paying attention.
4. **They calcify.** Made once, printed, put on a wall, never updated, cited for four years.
5. **They answer the wrong question.** They tell you *who*, when the design-relevant question is nearly always *in what situation*. The same person behaves completely differently rushing on a phone versus settled at a desk.

**What to use instead — three better tools:**

**A. Jobs and situations (Week 4).** Situational, and directly generative. "When X, I want Y, so I can Z."

**B. Behavioural archetypes.** If you want a person-shaped artifact, define it by *behaviour under constraint*, not demography:

> **The Deadline Deliverer** — works in bursts against external deadlines, uses the tool 3–4 times a month intensively, never explores, never reads release notes, needs everything findable on the day. Fails when: anything moved since last time.

That predicts design decisions. "Sarah, 32, likes yoga" does not.

**C. Anti-personas** — explicit statements of who you're *not* designing for. Often the most useful artifact in the room, because it makes the scoping decision visible and arguable rather than implicit.

**When personas do work:** large organisations where many people who will never meet a user need a shared shorthand; when they're grounded in real research, behaviour-first, dated, and revised. That's rare, and it's why the honest default is to skip them.

### SOURCE
NN/g on [Personas](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/persona/) for the mainstream defence, then Alan Cooper's *The Inmates Are Running the Asylum* for the original argument. Read both and form a position — you'll be asked about it in interviews.

### DRILL
Write **two behavioural archetypes** for your Studio B problem, based only on what your interviews actually showed. Each must be defined by behaviour under constraint, and each must include a "fails when" clause. Then write **one anti-persona** — who you are explicitly not designing for, and what you're giving up by excluding them.

## Day 5 — Usability testing: the cheapest high-value thing you will ever do

### CONCEPT

If you only ever adopt one research method for the rest of your career, adopt this one. It is fast, it is nearly free, and it is the only method that reliably makes a room full of people agree that a design has a problem.

**The core finding** (Nielsen, from Landauer & Nielsen's model): **five users find roughly 85% of usability problems.** The curve is steep at the start and flattens fast. Testing with five people twice, on two iterations, beats testing with ten once — by a lot. The point is not statistical confidence, it's *problem discovery*.

This is genuinely liberating, and it is also the fact most often used as an excuse to skip the method entirely, on the grounds that "we don't have time to recruit." You need five people and an hour each. You do have time.

**The protocol:**

1. **Write tasks, not questions.** "Buy a ticket for the 8pm show on Friday for two people" — not "what do you think of this page?" A task produces behaviour. A question produces opinion, and opinion is worthless here.
2. **Set the scene, then shut up.** Establish the context, then stop talking. Your job during the test is silence.
3. **Think-aloud.** Ask them to narrate. When they go quiet, prompt with "what are you thinking?" — never with "are you looking for the button?"
4. **Never help.** This is the hard part and it is *the entire method*. When someone struggles, every instinct says intervene. The struggle is the data. Sit on your hands. If they truly cannot proceed, note it as a task failure and move on.
5. **Answer questions with questions.** "What do you think it does?" not "it saves your draft."
6. **Record success, time, errors, and quotes.** Task completion is binary and worth tracking; the quotes are what you'll use to persuade people later.

**What makes it politically powerful, which is the underrated part:** watching a real person fail at your interface is *unarguable* in a way that a report is not. The single most effective thing you can do to change an organisation's design culture is get engineers and PMs to *watch a session live*. Ten minutes of watching a user hunt for a button they built converts more people than a year of advocacy. Your marketing background should tell you why: it's a demonstration, not a claim.

**Moderated vs unmoderated:**
- **Moderated** (you're there): richer, you can probe the *why*, essential for early or complex flows.
- **Unmoderated** (Maze, Lyssna — free tiers): faster, cheaper, scales, no probing. Good for comparing two versions or testing a specific known question.

Use moderated when you don't know what you're looking for, unmoderated when you do.

**On testing your own work:** you will be a worse moderator on your own designs, because you'll unconsciously help. Mitigate by writing the script in advance and reading it verbatim, and by consciously counting your interventions. If you can get someone else to moderate, do.

### SOURCE
Nielsen's [Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/), and Steve Krug's *Rocket Surgery Made Easy* — a short book that is essentially a how-to script for exactly this. Gomoll & Nicol's "Guidelines for user observation" is the classic short paper, on the [MIT 6.831 list](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/graphics/classes/6.831/readings.html).

### DRILL
Write a **usability test script** for the Studio A flow you tore down — three tasks, a scene-setting intro, and your think-aloud prompts. Then run it on one person. Count your interventions. The number will be embarrassing, which is the lesson.

## Day 6 — Studio B: run three real interviews

### CONCEPT

Today is fieldwork. No new concept — go and talk to humans.

**Before you start, the checklist:**
- [ ] Interview guide written, leading questions removed (Day 1)
- [ ] Recording set up, permission script ready
- [ ] Your dated hypothesis from Week 4 written down where you can't see it during the interviews
- [ ] You have decided, in advance, that you will not pitch

**Running the day:** three interviews is 2–3 hours including notes and probably won't fit one session. Spread across two or three days if needed — this is the one day of the course it's fine to expand. It's also the day most people quietly skip, and skipping it makes Weeks 6–8 hollow. Everything downstream is built on these three conversations.

**Immediately after each one**, before the next: five minutes of "what surprised me" notes. Surprise decays fast — within an hour you will have rationalised it into something you always knew.

### DRILL — Deliverables B1 + B2

**B1 — Three interviews.** Recorded, transcribed (use a model for transcription), with your post-interview surprise notes.

**B2 — Synthesis.**
- 30–50 atomic observations, affinity mapped, clusters named
- **Findings** section — what you observed, with counts ("two of three")
- **Insights** section — what it means and what follows, clearly separated from findings
- **One POV statement**, in the Week 4 Day 3 format, with a surprising and falsifiable "because" clause
- **The prediction check** — your Week 4 hypothesis vs what you found. What was wrong? Write it plainly.

*Time check: interviews 2–3 hrs (may span days), synthesis ~90 min.*

> `/product-design-course critique B2` — I'll check specifically whether your insights are actually derived from your observations or whether your hypothesis survived by being restated. That's the failure mode here and it's very hard to see in your own work.
