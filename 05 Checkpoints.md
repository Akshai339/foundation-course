# Checkpoints

Four assessments, one at the end of each phase. Not quizzes — **cold transfer tests**. You apply the phase's tools to something you haven't seen, under time pressure, then score yourself and compare against your own baseline.

← back to [[00 Start Here]]

---

## Why these exist, and why they're shaped this way

Reading a lesson and nodding is not learning. You will finish Week 3 feeling like you understand the two Gulfs, and then be unable to produce a decent diagnosis on demand. That gap — between recognition and production — is where most self-directed learning quietly fails, and it's invisible from the inside.

A checkpoint closes it by forcing three things:

1. **Cold material.** Something you haven't already thought about. Applying a framework to an example you chose is not evidence; applying it to something unfamiliar is.
2. **A time box.** Under time pressure you use what's actually available to you, not what you can reconstruct with effort. That's the honest measure.
3. **A comparison against your own past self.** This is the part that answers *"what progress have I made?"* — not a score in the abstract, but a measured delta against something you produced earlier in the course.

**They're also the only place you get a number.** Everything else in this course is qualitative. The five-axis profile below is the one instrument that shows a trajectory.

---

## The Progress Profile

At the end of every checkpoint you score yourself 1–5 on the same five axes from [[04 Rubrics and Self-Critique]]. Record it. Over four checkpoints you get a shape.

| Axis | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Framing** | Solves the problem as stated | Restated it more precisely | Found the real problem underneath, and can show why |
| **Structure** | Screens exist | Screens follow a flow | Flow derived from task analysis; states and branches complete |
| **Rationale** | "It felt right" | Can point at a principle | Can name the principle, the tradeoff, and what you gave up |
| **Craft** | Inconsistent | Consistent and clean | Systematic — spacing, type, colour derive from a stated logic |
| **Failure** | Happy path only | Errors handled | Empty / loading / error / partial / wrong / offline all designed |

**Your predicted Week 1 profile:** Framing 2 · Structure 2 · Rationale 2 · Craft 5 · Failure 1
**Target by Checkpoint 04:** everything ≥ 4.

> **The diagnostic that matters more than the total.** If **Craft** is still your highest score at Checkpoint 03, something has gone wrong — you've been retreating into the thing you're already good at. The interesting number is not the sum; it's whether the *shape* has flattened.

Score honestly. An inflated profile costs you the only feedback instrument you have.

# CHECKPOINT 01 — The Cold Diagnosis
### After Week 3 · 90 minutes · Phase 1: Foundations

**What it tests:** whether you can diagnose an interface mechanically, on demand, without the crutch of familiarity.

### Setup

Get someone else to pick the interface. Genuinely — text a friend and ask them to name an app and a specific task in it. If you pick it yourself you'll unconsciously choose something you've already formed opinions about, and the test becomes a recall exercise.

Failing that: open something you use *rarely* — a government portal, an insurance app, a booking system, an airline site, your bank's business section.

### The task — 60 minutes, timed

Produce a diagnosis of one flow. Constraints, all enforced:

- **Vocabulary lock.** Only Phase 1 and 2 terms: gulf of execution / evaluation, the seven action stages, affordance, signifier, constraint, mapping, conceptual model, directness, pre-attentive, the Gestalt laws, working memory / chunking / cognitive load, Fitts, Hick, slip, mistake, mode, and the ten heuristics. **If you can't express a problem in these terms, you haven't understood it — don't write it down.**
- **Minimum 12 findings.** Each tagged with its named principle.
- **Forced severity distribution.** At most two 4s. At least four 1s or 2s.
- **At least 5 findings must be structural**, not cosmetic. A structural finding is about what the flow *requires of the user* — sequence, memory, state, mapping. "The button is the wrong blue" is cosmetic. "Step 3 requires a value the user last saw on step 1" is structural.
- **One conceptual model statement** — three sentences on the story the product is telling about how it works, and where it leaks.

### The comparison — 30 minutes

Open your **Week 1 Day 6** annotation (Deliverable A1) and your **Week 3 Day 6** report (A3). Then:

1. Count the findings in A1 that were cosmetic vs structural. Write the ratio.
2. Do the same for today's diagnosis. Write the ratio.
3. **Write the delta in one paragraph.** Not "I improved" — specifically what kind of problem you can now see that you couldn't three weeks ago.
4. Re-read the prediction you wrote in Week 1 Day 6 about what was wrong with your Studio A flow. What did you not know you didn't know?

### The bar

- [ ] ≥ 60% of findings are structural
- [ ] Every finding carries a named principle
- [ ] Severity distribution forced, not clustered at 3–4
- [ ] At least one finding genuinely surprised you
- [ ] You can state the conceptual model and its leak

**If you miss the bar:** don't move on. Re-read Week 1 Day 2 (the Action Cycle) and Week 3 Day 4 (heuristic evaluation method), then run the checkpoint again on a different interface. This is the phase everything else is built on.

### Score the five axes

Expect Framing and Structure to still be low. Craft is not really tested here — score it against the *legibility of your report*, not the interface.

# CHECKPOINT 02 — The Framing Test
### After Week 6 · 2 hours · Phase 2: The Product Layer

**What it tests:** whether you can go from a vague stakeholder request to a structural spec — and, critically, whether you can tell what you *know* from what you're *assuming*.

### Setup

Pick **one** of these cold requests. Don't read ahead and choose the easiest; roll a die or take the one matching today's date mod 5.

> **1.** "Our support team is drowning. Can we add a chatbot to the help centre?"
>
> **2.** "Customers keep asking where their order is. We should send more emails."
>
> **3.** "The team wants a dashboard so everyone can see how the quarter is going."
>
> **4.** "Sign-ups are fine but nobody finishes setup. Can we make onboarding shorter?"
>
> **5.** "Our best customers say the product feels dated. We need a redesign."

Each is a real shape of request: a solution in disguise, a symptom mistaken for a cause, a request with an unexamined audience, a metric with an assumed diagnosis, and a vague quality complaint.

### The task — 2 hours, timed

Go from that sentence to a structural spec:

| | Output | ~Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ladder it** — at least 3 "what would that fix?" levels | 10 min |
| 2 | **The reframed problem**, in one sentence | 5 min |
| 3 | **Three job statements** — functional, emotional, social, full situation clause | 15 min |
| 4 | **One POV statement** — verb need, surprising and falsifiable "because" | 10 min |
| 5 | **Five HMWs** at different altitudes; mark the one you'd design against | 15 min |
| 6 | **Two pitches** — a 2-week and a 6-week appetite, with fat-marker sketches | 25 min |
| 7 | **Flow diagram** for the core goal — all branches, ≥3 failure paths, ≥2 entry points | 30 min |
| 8 | **State matrix skeleton** — screens × dimensions, `—` cells justified | 10 min |

### The catch — this is the actual test

**You cannot do research.** You have no users to talk to. So every claim you make about the user is an assumption.

**Mark every assumption explicitly**, inline, and for each one write what evidence would falsify it:

> *ASSUMPTION: managers check team state weekly rather than continuously.*
> *Falsified by: interview data showing daily checking, or analytics showing >3 sessions/week.*

**Minimum 8 marked assumptions.** This is the highest-value part of the checkpoint. The single most common failure of designers who've learned frameworks is running the framework confidently on invented inputs and mistaking the output for insight. If you produce a beautiful POV statement with zero marked assumptions, you have failed this checkpoint no matter how good the flow looks.

### The comparison

Open your **Week 4 Day 1** laddering exercise and your **Week 5 Day 6** synthesis (B2). Then:

1. How many levels deep did you ladder in Week 4? How many today?
2. In Week 5 you did the prediction check — something you believed turned out to be wrong. **Did you catch a similar over-confidence in yourself today, without research to correct you?**
3. One paragraph: what can you now see in a stakeholder request that you couldn't in Week 4?

### The bar

- [ ] The reframed problem is genuinely different from the request as stated
- [ ] ≥ 8 assumptions marked, each with a falsifier
- [ ] Flow has ≥ 3 failure paths and ≥ 2 entry points
- [ ] The two appetites differ in *scope*, not just in polish
- [ ] You can say what the 6-week version buys over the 2-week one, in one sentence

**If you miss the bar:** the most likely miss is assumptions. Re-run just steps 1–4 on a second request, marking assumptions as you go.

# CHECKPOINT 03 — The Handoff
### After Week 9 · 2 hours · Phase 3: The Craft Layer

**What it tests:** whether your system works *without you in the room*. This is the only real test of a design system, and almost nobody runs it on themselves.

### Setup

You need a second party. In order of preference: a designer who doesn't know your project · an engineer · a non-designer friend · Claude. Anyone but you.

### Part 1 — The build test (45 min)

Hand them your Studio B system — tokens, components, principles, the governance page — and a brief for **a screen you did not design**. Something adjacent but new. Examples: a settings page · a bulk-edit view · a search-results state · an account-deletion flow · a notification centre.

Then **leave them alone.** No answering questions. Write down every question they *wanted* to ask — each one is a hole in your documentation.

Evaluate what comes back on three counts:

| Test | Pass |
|---|---|
| **Autonomy** — could they build it without asking you anything? | 0 blocking questions |
| **Fidelity** — did they use your tokens, or invent values? | < 3 invented values |
| **Coherence** — does it look like it belongs to the same product? | Yes, to a third party's eye |

The **Autonomy** count is the one that matters. A system that requires its author present is not a system; it's a habit.

### Part 2 — The torture test (45 min)

Take your densest screen. Apply the full pressure set from Week 7 Day 5:

- Longest realistic string in every field, then an implausibly long one
- A record with every optional field null
- Lists at 0, 1, 2, and 200 items
- Numbers at 0, 7, 1,247, 1,300,000 — does the column jump?
- +30% string length (stand-in for German)
- 320px wide, 2560px wide, and 200% browser zoom
- An inline error appearing mid-layout — does anything below it move?

**Count the failures.** Then fix them. Then count how many you'd have shipped.

### Part 3 — The motion spec (30 min)

Take one transition — the most important one in your flow. Specify it completely, in tokens only:

- What moves, what stays, what leads, what follows
- Duration and easing token per element
- Stagger offsets, and the cap
- What happens on interruption at 40%
- The reduced-motion variant

**If you need a value your token set doesn't have, that's a finding**, not a licence to invent one. Either the token set is incomplete or the animation is wrong.

### The comparison

1. **Week 7 Day 2:** you counted the unique spacing values in one of your screens. What was the number? Count again now.
2. **Week 1:** could a stranger have built a screen from anything you'd produced then?
3. One paragraph: what does your system now do that your taste used to do?

### The bar

- [ ] 0 blocking questions from the second party
- [ ] < 3 invented values in what they built
- [ ] Every torture-test failure fixed
- [ ] Motion spec'd entirely in tokens, with an interruption case and a reduced-motion variant
- [ ] Unique spacing values ≤ 8

**If you miss the bar:** it will be Autonomy. Read what they wanted to ask, write those answers into the system, and re-run Part 1 with a different person and a different screen.

# CHECKPOINT 04 — The Defence
### After Week 12 · 2 hours · Phase 4: The Professional Layer

**What it tests:** whether the work survives contact with other people — and whether *you* survive it without becoming defensive or capitulating.

### Part 1 — The critique (60 min)

Present Studio C to **two people, one of whom is not a designer.** Run the protocol from Week 12 Day 3 properly:

- You frame first, two minutes: the job, the constraint, and the *specific* thing you want help with
- Silent review, five minutes, before anyone speaks
- **Three passes in order — structural, then interaction, then visual.** Enforce it. When someone opens with "the type feels heavy," say "hold that for pass three."
- Questions before opinions
- Capture everything verbatim

**Your only job in the room is to understand.** Write every point down before responding to any of them. Do not explain. Do not defend. Notice the moment you most want to — and write down which point it was, because that's the one you're least sure about.

### Part 2 — The response (45 min)

Now, alone, write the response document:

1. **Everything I heard** — the raw list
2. **Classified** — for each: is it a *problem*, a *solution*, or a *preference*? Solutions get translated back into the problem underneath.
3. **What I'll act on**, and what changes
4. **What I won't act on, and why.** At least one. A designer who accepts every piece of feedback has no position; a designer who rejects it all has no collaborators. **The reasoning is the deliverable here.**
5. **What I got wrong** — one honest paragraph

### Part 3 — The rationale under pressure (15 min)

Pick the **three most significant decisions** in Studio C. For each, in three sentences: the decision, the tradeoff, and **what you gave up**.

If you cannot name what you gave up on a decision, you didn't make a decision — you made a guess that happened to work. This is the single fastest signal of seniority available to you, and it's the thing that will be tested in every interview and every design review for the rest of your career.

### The final comparison

This is the last measurement of the course. Take your time.

1. **Score all five axes.** Plot against your Week 1 predicted profile: Framing 2 · Structure 2 · Rationale 2 · Craft 5 · Failure 1.
2. **Has the shape flattened?** If Craft is still highest, name where you retreated.
3. **Re-read the 200 words you wrote on Week 1, Day 1** — "the last time I designed something that looked right and worked badly." Write what you'd *now* say was actually wrong with it. This paragraph is the clearest possible measure of the twelve weeks.
4. **Read your twelve answers** to the recurring weekly question — *where did I default to visual craft instead of the harder thinking?* Look for the pattern. **That pattern is your development plan for next year.**

### The bar

- [ ] You ran three passes in order and did not let it start at visual
- [ ] You did not defend in the room
- [ ] You declined at least one piece of feedback, with a stated reason
- [ ] You named what you gave up on all three decisions
- [ ] All five axes ≥ 4
- [ ] The profile has flattened — Craft is no longer the outlier

```bash

```

Claude will set the cold material, hold you to the time box, refuse to let you grade yourself generously, and record the profile into `PROGRESS.md`. Ask it to:

- **Pick the interface** for Checkpoint 01 (it won't pick one you've mentioned)

- **Run the critique** for Checkpoint 04, in three enforced passes, as a skeptical non-designer

The one thing it will do that you can't do alone: **tell you when your self-score is generous.** Ask for that explicitly.
